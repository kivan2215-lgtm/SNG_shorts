A passenger jet lost its engines, one after another. All four. A strange blue glow appeared outside the windows. The crew had no idea what was happening.

June twenty-fourth, nineteen eighty-two. A Boeing flies over Indonesia. Smoke enters the cabin. The aircraft can no longer maintain altitude. The pilots try to restart the engines. Again and again. Nothing works. Below them is the ocean.

But fuel was not the problem. The aircraft had entered a cloud of volcanic ash. At night, the crew had not seen it. Their weather radar did not reveal the dry ash either. Inside the hot engines, particles melted and stuck to components. Airflow was disrupted. The engines flamed out.

Then something unexpected happened. As the aircraft descended, it left the ash cloud. The deposits cooled, hardened, and broke away. After roughly fifteen minutes without thrust, the crew restarted the engines. One soon had to be shut down again.

But the ordeal was not over. Ash had damaged the windscreens. The pilots could barely see the runway. They still managed to land in Jakarta. Everyone on board survived. It was not a massive storm that stopped four engines. It was tiny particles the crew had never even seen.
