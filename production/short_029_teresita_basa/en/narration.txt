Police could not find a woman's killer for six months. Then a doctor came to the detectives. He said, “My wife speaks in the dead woman's voice.” And he gave them a name.

Chicago, nineteen seventy-seven. A fire broke out in Teresita Basa's apartment. Firefighters found her body. She had been stabbed, and the fire was meant to hide the crime. The investigation stalled.

Months later, the doctor's wife, Remibias Chua, began acting strangely. Her husband said she spoke in another voice three times and called herself Teresita. The couple hesitated to tell police. Eventually, she named Allan Showery, a worker at the same hospital. She claimed he had taken the victim's jewelry.

Detectives checked the name. At Showery's girlfriend's home, they found Teresita's pearl ring and jade pendant. Those were real clues. Showery confessed, then recanted. His first trial ended without a verdict. Later, he pleaded guilty and received a fourteen-year sentence.

But what happened to Remibias? She also worked at the hospital and might have learned something in an ordinary way. The source of her information was never established. The strange account led police to a suspect. The jewelry provided the evidence.
