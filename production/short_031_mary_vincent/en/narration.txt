In court, a woman raised her metal prosthesis and pointed at the defendant. But he was on trial for killing another woman. Why was her testimony so important?

California, nineteen seventy-eight. Fifteen-year-old Mary Vincent was hitchhiking. Lawrence Singleton stopped his van. He drove her to an isolated place, sexually assaulted her, then severed both her arms below the elbows. He threw Mary into a ravine and left her to die. But she survived, reached the road, and found people who helped her.

Singleton was caught and sentenced to fourteen years in prison. But in nineteen eighty-seven, he was released on parole after serving about eight years. For Mary, the fear did not end with the trial.

Ten years later, in Florida, Singleton murdered thirty-one-year-old Roxanne Hayes, a mother of three. A witness saw the attack and went for help. The following year, Mary faced Singleton in court again. This is the moment in the photograph. She pointed at him with her prosthesis and described what he had done to her twenty years earlier.

Her testimony came during the penalty phase. This time, Singleton was sentenced to death. But Roxanne could not be brought back. Even the full fourteen-year sentence would have ended before her murder. So how do we determine whether someone who committed such a crime is safe to release?
