A submarine sank to the seabed. People were still alive inside. To reach them, rescuers had to open a hatch underwater. But how could they do that without flooding the people they came to save?

May twenty-third, nineteen thirty-nine. Off the American coast, the new submarine Squalus begins a test dive. An air induction valve fails to close. Seawater pours in. The aft compartments flood. Twenty-six people die. The others manage to seal off the water.

Thirty-three people are trapped below the surface. The depth: two hundred forty-three feet. Then a steel rescue chamber is lowered toward the submarine. But simply placing it over the hatch will not be enough.

A diver attaches a guide cable. The chamber is pulled down onto the hatch. A rubber ring forms a watertight seal against the hull. Water is removed from the chamber's lower compartment. A dry passage now connects the two vessels. Only then can the hatches open. People climb inside, and the chamber carries them toward the surface.

It takes four trips. All thirty-three people who survived the flooding are brought up alive. It is the first rescue from a sunken submarine using this method. They could not escape on their own. So rescuers brought the way out down to them.
