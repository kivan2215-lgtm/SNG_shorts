A woman accused her doctor of sexual assault. But the DNA did not match. Not once. Not twice. Was she wrong? The answer was hiding right beneath his skin.

Canada. Nineteen ninety-two. A patient said her doctor had drugged and raped her. Police collected evidence and took his blood. No match. She demanded another test. The same result. Eventually, the case was closed.

But she refused to give up. A private investigator obtained lip balm from the doctor's car. It carried traces of saliva. This time, the DNA matched the evidence. How could one man produce two different results? Another victim later came forward. Investigators reopened the case. New samples, including hair, linked the doctor to the crime.

At trial, the reason for those earlier results emerged. He had hidden a tube of someone else's blood beneath his skin. During the earlier tests, blood had been drawn from that tube. He had not fooled the analysis. He had switched what was being analyzed.

In nineteen ninety-nine, John Schneeberger was convicted of sexual assault and obstruction of justice. He received six years in prison. His first victim had fought for justice for seven years. Is six years enough for sexual assault and trying to deceive the justice system?
